# Diversification

The only free lunch in investing: reducing risk without giving up expected return — but only
if the holdings are genuinely *different*. Owning many things is not the same as diversifying.

## Correlation is the whole game
- Diversification works because uncorrelated assets don't all fall together — one zigs while
  another zags, smoothing the ride.
- **Five correlated names is one bet in disguise.** Five megacap tech stocks, or five oil
  producers, move together — in a drawdown they all fall at once. Count that as *one*
  position's worth of risk, not five.
- In a crisis, correlations converge toward 1 (things fall together). Real diversification
  needs assets that behave differently *in stress*, not just on calm days.

## Concentration limits
- Cap any single position (`max_position_weight`) so no one name can dominate outcomes.
- Cap any single **sector** and **theme** too — a 6% position in each of six semiconductor
  names is a ~36% chip bet. Aggregate exposure is what matters, not line-item count.
- The flip side: **over-diversification** (50+ tiny positions) just recreates the index at
  higher cost and effort. A focused, uncorrelated 15–30 is usually plenty for individuals;
  a broad low-cost index fund is diversification in a single line.

## Spread across sectors and factors
- **Sectors:** don't let one sector (see `sector_rotation`) drive the portfolio; a regime
  shift can punish a whole sector at once.
- **Factors:** growth vs value, large vs small, cyclical vs defensive, domestic vs
  international, and different market-cap and geography exposures diversify differently than
  sectors alone.
- **Asset classes:** stocks, bonds, cash, and real assets respond to different macro regimes
  (see `macro_regime`) — the deepest diversification is across asset classes, not just stocks.

## Rebalancing discipline
- Over time winners grow into oversized, concentrated positions and quietly raise portfolio
  risk. **Rebalancing** trims them back to target weights and tops up laggards.
- It is a systematic "sell high, buy low" — mechanical, not emotional. Rebalance on a
  schedule (e.g. quarterly/annually) or on a drift threshold (e.g. a weight off target by
  a set band), not on impulse.
- Mind taxes and costs; in taxable accounts, prefer rebalancing with new contributions.

## Discipline
- **Diversify the risk, not just the tickers.** Ask "what would make all of these fall
  together?" — if there's a common answer, you're less diversified than you think.
- Diversification limits blow-ups and smooths returns; it does **not** guarantee gains and
  will cap upside in a runaway single name. That trade-off is the point.
